# Build the glove hand as one piece

The potato hands read as separate parts because they are built and inked as separate parts: the palm ball, every finger capsule, every knuckle ball, the thumb and the wrist cone are each their own mesh with their own ink outline and their own shading. Wherever one part enters another the ink draws a ring roughly 4 pixels wide, and the arm is a different mesh again. Cartoonists and 3D modellers avoid this the same way. They start from one mitten-shaped mass and cut the fingers out of it. In 3D they build the hand and forearm as one continuous surface: the palm runs halfway up each finger before the gap between fingers starts, and the thumb grows out of a pad at the palm's lower corner. **The recommended build keeps today's simple shapes, but only as an invisible skeleton, and melts them into one skin with code.** Each shape becomes a distance field. Each finger joins the palm, and the palm joins the forearm, through a rounded fillet. Neighbouring fingers stay apart with a hard join. The result is turned into a single mesh every frame. Benchmarks run in this container put that at about **50–150 ms of CPU per hand**, small next to the 1.7 s per frame a render already takes, and the ink then draws only the hand's real outline and folds. If the per-frame mesh shimmers in a test strip, the fallback is to build the mesh once and bend it with bones. On animation the craft sources agree on four things. The index finger acts alone and the other fingers move as one group. The smallest finger closes first. The wrist is never straight. A thin prop such as a pencil or straw is held pad-to-pad or against the side of a finger, high up, so it shows on both sides of the glove. Two choices belong to Jonny. The current "glove" hand has four fingers plus a thumb (five digits), so going to three fingers plus a thumb changes the look. So does choosing between a cuffed and a seamless wrist.

## Every part draws its own ink ring, so the hand reads as a kit

The engine's glove (`engine/page/potato.js`, `_mitten`, type `"glove"`) is a scaled palm sphere, four fingers made of two capsules each plus a "round knuckle" ball at the root and at the middle joint, a thumb capsule, and a short cone for the heel of the hand. The arm is a separate tapered tube that is rebuilt every frame and narrows to a wrist "before the mitten". The fist used on staffs and handles is a third set of parts that grows in while the open fingers shrink away. Each of those meshes goes through three.js's `OutlineEffect`, which redraws the mesh inside-out, pushed outward along its normals by a thickness of 0.0042 (`engine/page/styles.js`; `node_modules/three/examples/jsm/effects/OutlineEffect.js`). That works out to an ink band of about **4 px** on a 1080×1920 frame.

That band explains the seams. Where a finger capsule sinks into the palm sphere, the finger's pushed-out shell sits slightly in front of the palm's surface along the line where the two meet. Nothing covers it, so the ink draws a ring there. Every knuckle ball adds two more rings. Artists working with inverted-hull outlines report the same symptom: "close or intersecting vertices, or separate pieces on top of each other" make the outline "look like it's exploding" ([Polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/comment/2732547/)). Arc System Works, whose Guilty Gear games set the standard for anime-style 3D ink, describes hulls on mismatched surfaces as "segregated and disjointed" ([ASW Academy, Guilty Gear Toon Line lecture, pp. 57–62](https://docswell.com/s/ASW_Academy/5LVY67-GG-Toonline-Eng)). The toon shading has the same problem in another form. The light-to-shadow edge restarts on every primitive instead of running across the finger roots, so each finger looks stuck on even where no ink shows. Turning off the outline on the inner parts doesn't fix it, because it also removes their real silhouettes.

Arc System Works' production tricks show what a fix has to provide. They control line width per vertex (0 = no line, 0.5 = standard, 1.0 = double) and set it to zero in valleys, naming **glove and sleeve rims** specifically, so no floating line appears where two surfaces meet. They also give the outline its own smoothed normals, separate from the lighting normals, so the shell never splits at a hard edge ([ASW lecture pp. 49–50, 71–76](https://docswell.com/s/ASW_Academy/5LVY67-GG-Toonline-Eng)). Both tricks assume one connected mesh with shared vertices across the join. Neither can be applied to a pile of separate capsules. The knuckle balls, added so a curled finger would read as a soft rolled glove, are the worst offenders: each adds a bump to the outline and a ring to the ink. The seam is therefore not a missing detail. It comes from how the hand is put together, and adding more parts makes it worse.

## Cartoonists start from a mitten and cut the fingers out

Every construction method found starts from the whole hand as one mass. Preston Blair taught drawing the hand as a mitten first and adding fingers afterwards ("figure out the big shapes first") ([Gurney Journey](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2012/03/drawing-cartoon-hands.html)). John Kricfalusi's animation lesson says never to start from the fingers: pose two masses, the palm and the finger block, then cut the fingers into the second one ([John K Stuff, Lesson 5a](http://johnkstuff.blogspot.com/2006/06/animation-school-lesson-hands.html)). Four digits became the convention for related reasons. Early studio characters were built from circles, and five fingers on a round palm looked like "a bunch of bananas". Each extra finger also cost drawings in every frame. Three digits read as alien or mutant, so four is the friendly count ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/posts/why-cartoon-characters-have-four-fingers)). For a round potato body the circle argument decides it: a round palm has room for about three plump fingers and a thumb before they crowd into a fan.

The most numeric construction source is a Clip Studio tutorial. It covers realistic-to-stylized hands, so its ratios are starting points, not studio rules. It models the palm as a "toast" shape whose curved top peaks at the middle finger, with palm width ≈ palm height ≈ middle-finger length. Fingers are thickest at the first joint and shaped like slightly squashed jelly beans, not cylinders. Even when held together they angle toward a common point, and the gaps are uneven. The key line for this question: **seen from the back, fingers don't separate from the palm until about halfway between the knuckle and the first joint.** The palm mass continues up into the finger roots. The thumb's root sits at the lower corner of the palm, shares the palm pad, and is joined to the palm by webbing up to its first joint ([Clip Studio Tips, "Expressive hands"](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/11070)). Those two features, the shared finger roots and the webbed thumb pad, are what make fingers look grown rather than glued. The current glove has neither: its fingers are capsules pushed into the rim of the palm sphere, and its thumb is a capsule on a pivot.

Simplifying a hand means removing detail, with one exception. More joint lines and creases make a hand look older, and stylized hands show joints with shading, not lines ([Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/11070)). Eric Goldberg, as quoted on Gurney's blog, still keeps a little detail at bony landmarks such as the bend of the thumb, so a simple hand reads as a hand and not a rubber hose ([Gurney Journey](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2012/03/drawing-cartoon-hands.html)). Tutorials list the same failures: uniform parallel finger tubes, clone fingers, a square palm, and a thumb treated "as an entity of its own" ([People's Pharmacy mirror](https://library.peoplespharmacy.com/draw-human-hands), a low-quality page but consistent with Clip Studio). For a navy toon glove this means no creases, nails or knuckle bumps, and one shading band that crosses the whole hand. Where a finger bends, the knuckle's roundness comes from the surface thickening slightly, not from a ball.

The wrist has two clean solutions and one bad one. Cartoon gloves began as a readability device: Disney gave Mickey white gloves because black hands vanished against his black body ([D23](https://d23.com/ask-dave/marie-serris-france/)), and Miyamoto gave Mario gloves so his movements read while jumping ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/1)). A glove with a cuff makes the wrist a deliberate break: a flared ring, a little wider than the forearm, in a different value. Without a cuff, the forearm has to narrow into the wrist and widen into the heel of the palm as one surface, with no step in width and no ink at the join. A separate hand butted onto the arm in the same colour, with no cuff, is the half-way state, and it is what reads as a seam. That is the engine's state today. The same history carries a warning for dark navy hands: when a hand crosses a body of similar value it needs a rim or value contrast, or it disappears, as Mickey's did.

The table turns the sources into starting values for a three-finger-plus-thumb glove. The ratios marked as proposals are this report's inferences from the drawing sources, to be tuned on stills.

| Feature | Starting value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Palm | one rounded wedge, thicker at the heel, knuckle line an arc peaking at the middle finger, outer root lower than the index root | Clip Studio "toast" palm; pentagon palm with sloped pinky side |
| Finger length | about palm length, shorter for a chubby read | Clip Studio ratio; current glove already uses "about as long as the palm is deep" |
| Finger root | palm surface runs up about half of the first segment before the cleft starts | Clip Studio back view |
| Finger shape | thickest just past the root, tapering to a round tip; axes converge when closed, fan toward the wrist when open | Clip Studio |
| Gaps | uneven: index a little apart, the two outer fingers close or touching | Clip Studio; AnimSchool grouping |
| Thumb | cone from the lower inner corner on a shared pad, webbed to its first joint, about finger thickness, set lower and angled out | Clip Studio |
| Wrist | narrower than both forearm and palm, then flaring over 1–2 ring widths into the heel and thumb pad (or a cuff that owns the break) | proposal, from the glove history and topology advice |
| Size | about a third to half of head height, oversized so gestures read on a phone | proposal; no sourced ratio for mascot hands was found |

## One blended skin, generated by code, removes the seams

### The recommended build

Practitioners model a stylized hand as one mesh continuous with the forearm. They block the palm as a box, extrude or merge the digits from it, add extra edge loops at every joint, and keep awkward vertices off the wrist and knuckles ([Highend3D creature hand](https://www.highend3d.com/tutorials/creature-hand-tutorial-by-erin-nicholson); [Blender Artists](https://blenderartists.org/t/best-way-to-topologise-this-hand/604296)). A procedural engine can get the same single surface without hand-modelling, by **describing each part as a signed distance field (SDF) and blending them**. A distance field is a function that says how far any point is from a shape. Two of them can be merged with a "smooth minimum", which fills the corner between them with a rounded fillet. Inigo Quilez's reference formulas cover the primitives needed. The exact round cone (a capsule with different radii at its two ends) is the right piece for a tapered finger segment or forearm. His ellipsoid is only an approximation, which distorts blend widths, so the palm is better built from two fat round cones than from today's scaled sphere ([iq, distance functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions/)).

The choice of smooth minimum matters. Quilez's polynomial family (quadratic, cubic, quartic, circular) leaves shapes untouched once they are more than the blend distance *k* apart, and *k* "matches exactly the maximum inflation" a join adds. Exponential and similar blends distort shapes everywhere ([iq, smooth minimum](https://iquilezles.org/articles/smin/)). Media Molecule's Dreams, the largest shipped SDF modelling tool, used the same quadratic soft-min, which "has no effect once abs(a−b) > r" ([Evans, SIGGRAPH 2015](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/AlexEvans_SIGGRAPH-2015-sml.pdf)). That clamped behaviour lets a fingertip keep its exact shape while its root melts into the palm. The circular variant is the only one that gives an exactly round fillet where two shapes meet at right angles, which makes it the cleanest choice for a finger meeting the palm ([iq, smooth minimum](https://iquilezles.org/articles/smin/)).

The structure that gives "grown, not glued" fingers without webbing comes from that formula. The research notes worked it out as follows. With the quadratic blend, two parallel fingers with a gap *g* bridge into webbing exactly when **g < 2k**. So each pair of shapes gets its own blend distance instead of one global value. The palm's two halves blend with k ≈ 0.6–0.8 × finger radius. The palm blends into the forearm with k ≈ 0.8 × finger radius, which makes the wrist fillet. Each finger's segments blend with a small k ≈ 0.15 × radius, only to soften the inside of a bent knuckle. Each finger blends into the palm with k ≈ 0.5 × finger radius. **Fingers combine with each other using a hard minimum**, so they never melt together, and two touching fingers keep a real crease that the ink should draw. The test hand used k = 8, 10, 2 and 6 mm with finger radii near 12 mm, close to the current glove's 16–17.5 mm radii. The smooth minimum is not associative, so the order of operations must be fixed in code (palm first, then each finger against the palm). Otherwise the shape depends on list order, which would break the repo's "same storyboard, same video" rule.

To render it, the field is turned into triangles every frame with **naive surface nets**, a meshing method that makes about four times fewer vertices than marching cubes and ran faster in published tests ([0fps](https://0fps.net/2012/07/12/smooth-voxel-terrain-part-2/)). It takes about 80 lines of JavaScript. The notes recommend these settings: a sampling grid fixed to the palm, not the world, so a moving arm doesn't slide the surface through the grid; cells about one fifth of the thinnest fingertip radius (2–3 mm here); one Newton step per vertex to snap it onto the true surface; and normals taken from the field's gradient with Quilez's four-sample method ([iq, SDF normals](https://iquilezles.org/articles/normalsSDF/)) rather than from the triangles. Smooth gradient normals keep the toon terminator clean and give OutlineEffect an unbroken shell to push out. The benchmarks measured in this container (4 vCPU, Node 22, a 14-part test hand; scripts in `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-viral-engine/113885ae-d80b-5f3c-b080-dda23fa6260c/scratchpad/bench/`) found an optimised field pass of **42 ms at 3 mm and 93 ms at 2 mm**. An unoptimised full field-plus-mesh pass took 86–364 ms at 4–2 mm. That gives 5k–38k triangles per hand, comfortable for SwiftShader to draw. The engine already rebuilds each arm tube every frame (`_tube` in `potato.js`), so a per-frame hand mesh fits the existing pattern.

Joining the hand to the arm needs one more decision, and the sources don't settle it. The cleanest option puts the forearm inside the field, from elbow to fingertips, with the upper-arm tube ending inside it. The cheapest first step is a forearm stub in the field, about 5 cm long, whose radius and direction match the arm tube where the tube ends inside it, so the two surfaces coincide instead of crossing. Both are this report's proposals. When the hand rolls about the forearm, the rig practice is to spread the twist along the forearm rather than turning a rigid hand on a still tube. Riggers use 2–3 twist segments that each take a share of the roll ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/3ds-max-animation-and-rigging/rotate-wrist-and-have-forearm-twist-automatically-cat-rig/m-p/8986259); [Blender Rigify](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/addons/rigify/rig_types/limbs.html)). In this engine that means rotating forearm ring *i* of *n* by θ·i/n.

One practical point keeps the checker working. The contact checks in `engine/page/stage.js` measure "every visible part of a hand (palm, wrist, fingers, thumb, the fist's knuckles), as balls along its own axis". If the old capsules stay in the scene as invisible proxies, the field is built from them and the checker keeps measuring them, so hand-in-prop, thin-prop-hidden and face-hidden all go on working unchanged. The skin is then a drawing of the skeleton, and the skeleton is still what gets measured.

### How the alternatives compare

| Approach | Seamless? | Cost per frame here | Main risk | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SDF blend, meshed every frame (surface nets) | yes, fillets follow every pose | ~50–150 ms CPU per hand (local benchmark) | triangles change as fingers move, so the outline could twitch by a pixel (not yet measured) | **recommended first build** |
| SDF blend meshed once in the rest pose, then bent with bones (`SkinnedMesh`) | yes, fixed topology, cannot pop | near zero | three.js has only linear blend skinning, which pinches inside a bent knuckle and twists like a "candy wrapper" at the wrist ([three.js docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/SkinnedMesh.html); [Kavan et al.](https://dcgi.fel.cvut.cz/en/publications/2007/kavan-i3d-sdq/)) | **fallback** if the per-frame mesh shimmers |
| Raymarch the SDF in a shader | yes, perfect | ~111 ms for a 300 px hand, ~497 ms for 600 px, per pass under SwiftShader (local benchmark) | loses OutlineEffect, needs custom depth, shadow and ink passes | reference renderer and hero close-ups only |
| three.js `MarchingCubes` metaballs | yes | 28–51 ms (local benchmark) | summed fields make fingers swell and bridge; fixed [-1,1] box and fixed bounding sphere ([three.js source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/jsm/objects/MarchingCubes.js)) | poor fit for close fingers |
| Swept tube mesh (B-Mesh, Blender Skin modifier) | yes | low | the palm is a 4–5-way branch, where the Skin modifier admits "complicated topology, some of which may overlap" ([Blender manual](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/modeling/modifiers/generate/skin.html)); no JS implementation exists | more code for no gain |
| Keep primitives, patch the ink | no | none | zero-width lines and smoothed outline normals need a shared mesh; hiding inner outlines loses real silhouettes | stopgap only |

On the blended mesh the ink appears only where the surface turns away from the camera, and at real folds such as a curled finger in front of the palm. One artifact needs watching. In concavities narrower than the line width on screen, such as inside a fist or between fingers, the inverted shell can poke through the front surface. Keeping finger gaps at least 2k wide and checking fists in close-up covers it. If the per-frame mesh does shimmer, the baked-and-skinned fallback needs 2–3 bones per finger with soft weights across each joint, plus a correcting shape for a full curl. three.js supports morph targets and skinning together.

## Grips read when the index acts alone and the prop stays rigid

### A small pose library built on "virtual fingers"

AnimSchool's posing guides give the base rules. A default rig hand is "flat and rigid". A relaxed hand gets its curve from the first two joints, and the fingertip joint is kept for tension (a clench, a grasp). Fingers are grouped, with the index floating slightly apart. A fist needs the index raised or angled and uneven knuckle spacing ([AnimSchool, Hand Posing Basics](https://blog.animschool.edu/2025/05/09/hand-posing-basics/); [AnimSchool, Posing Hands](https://blog.animschool.edu/2025/06/13/posing-hands-for-animation-2/)). Robotics research on real grasps maps human holds onto a three-finger glove. Feix et al. merged 22 taxonomies into 33 grasps, and 17 prototypes cover 83.4% of observed grasp time. They also define "virtual fingers": fingers that push the same way and move together act as one unit ([Feix et al. 2016](https://www.eng.yale.edu/grablab/pubs/Feix_THMS2016.pdf)). For a glove that settles which finger does what. Glove finger 1 is the index, the actor that points, pinches and lies along a handle. Glove fingers 2 and 3 are "the rest", one group with a small stagger. The angles below are this report's starting values to tune on stills, not sourced numbers.

| Pose | Index (f1) | Outer fingers (f2, f3) | Thumb | Wrist |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Relaxed | knuckle 15°, middle 20°, tip 5° | 20/25/5° and 25/30/5°, f3 slightly over f2 | ~30° off the palm, lightly bent | 10–15° bent back or toward the little finger |
| Fist | a little less curled or angled | fully curled, tip joints engaged | across the middle segments | knuckle line on an arc |
| Point | straight, slight hyperextension | curled as a fist | tucked over f2 | follows the line of action |
| Open / presenting | apart from the others | close together, slight back-curve | out | bent back |
| Cup / carry | all three mildly curled together | (same) | up the side | palm faces the load |
| Handle, staff, bat (medium wrap) | wraps round, the thumb closes over it | wrap round | over f1 | forearm square to the shaft |
| Thin prop (pencil, straw) | pad on the prop | curled more than f1 so they clear it | pad opposes f1 | prop gripped in its upper third |

The handle row matches the engine's existing fist, and medium wrap is the most-used human grasp: **12.7% of grasps and 23.8% of grasp time** in Feix's data. Power grasps lock the object to the hand, so "all object motion comes from the arm". Precision grasps let the fingers move the object ([Feix et al. 2016](https://www.eng.yale.edu/grablab/pubs/Feix_THMS2016.pdf)). For the engine that means a power grasp for anything carried as a unit, and finger motion only on a twirl or flick. This is the same rule as CLAUDE.md's "a held object is rigid in the hand".

### Thin objects: pad to pad or against the side of a finger, held high

Real people rarely hold a thin object between fingertips. In Feix's data the **tip pinch is 0.3% of grasps**. The palmar (pad-to-pad) pinch is 3.9%, the lateral grasp (thumb pad against the side of the index, as with a key) 8.9%, and the tripod 8.4%. The literature also over-represents pinches compared with real use ([Feix et al. 2016](https://www.eng.yale.edu/grablab/pubs/Feix_THMS2016.pdf)). A thumb-on-fingertip pinch therefore reads as fussy. The pad-to-pad pinch already in the grip library, where the thumb and index pads oppose (Jonny's straw brief, `potato.js` `_pose`), is the realistic version and stays: the brief wins. The research adds two refinements that fit it. The outer fingers curl further than the index, so they clear a prop that runs on below the fingertips, which the engine's `lean` parameter already does. For a pencil in use, the writing tripod (thumb, index and the side of f2, with f3 curled into the palm) rests the shaft in the web of the thumb, so it shows above and below the glove. That satisfies the thin-prop-hidden rule and CLAUDE.md's "grip it high". A blended hand helps here too. Finger pads that press slightly into a prop, which the contact rules allow, no longer draw a ring of ink round the dent.

### Timing at 30 fps: reach, close, hold, lift, release

The reach is a chain. On a reach for keys, the upper arm rises, the forearm pivots, the wrist bends back and the fingers curl last, each part starting before the previous one ends. Moving every part to the end pose together is named as the common mistake ([Wikibooks, Overlapping action](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Traditional_Principles_of_Animation/Overlapping_action)). When a hand closes, "the smallest finger closes first and opens last" ([AnimSchool](https://blog.animschool.edu/2025/06/13/posing-hands-for-animation-2/)). A pick-up uses "a crisp 3–5 frame contact" and then a two-stage lift: a quick break off the surface, then a slower carry that eases to its target ([Sunstrike Studios](https://sunstrikestudios.com/en/blog/timing_in_animation/)). Animation Mentor's parent-constraint tutorial attaches the prop on the contact frame rather than blending it over several frames. Only a search snippet of it could be read ([Animation Mentor](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/tutorial-animating-character-prop-interaction/)). Combined with the repo's own eyes-lead rule, these give a proposed schedule. The eyes go to the prop about 4 frames ahead. Shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers each start 2–3 frames after the previous one. In the last third of the reach the hand opens to about the prop's width plus 30–50%. It closes in 3–5 frames, f3, f2 and f1 one frame apart. The prop attaches on a single frame. The hand holds still for 2–4 frames, breaks off the surface in 3–4 frames, then eases into the carry. Release runs in reverse: f1 and the thumb open first, and the hand moves away only after the fingers have cleared the prop.

### Appeal, readability and the fist swap

Appeal comes from breaking symmetry and from lag. "A perfectly straight wrist breaks the illusion of life" ([AnimSchool](https://blog.animschool.edu/2025/06/13/posing-hands-for-animation-2/)). Joints break in succession, upper arm, elbow, wrist, fingers, an idea credited to Art Babbitt's lectures at Richard Williams' studio ([AnimSchool, Follow Through and Overlap](https://blog.animschool.edu/2024/04/19/follow-through-and-overlap-animation-tips/); [Animation Apprentice](https://animationapprentice.blogspot.com/2019/04/what-is-successive-breaking-of-joints.html)). Two hands doing the same thing look mechanical. Cartoony styles can twin "only when you have a reason", such as a shocked hands-up ([Animation Mentor, via snippet](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/twinning-and-why-you-should-usually-avoid-it/)). In procedural terms, the outer finger always curls most (curl × 1, 1.15–1.25, 1.3–1.5). The f1–f2 gap is 1.5–2 times the f2–f3 gap. The two hands are offset by 1–2 frames and a few degrees. Free fingers lag 5–15° when the wrist starts or stops fast and settle over 3–6 frames, while a gripping hand never lags because its prop is rigid. These numbers are proposals; no source gives finger drag angles.

At phone size a glove is a few dozen pixels, so finger acting reads only in close shots. Keith Lango's staging advice is to show a gesture only when the story needs it, and to gesture with the hand farther from camera so it doesn't cover the body ([Keith Lango notes](https://crowlena.wordpress.com/2015/12/18/keith-lango-posing-weight-and-staging-hands/)). In fast or stylized moves, fingers group into one silhouette with less negative space between them ([AnimSchool](https://blog.animschool.edu/2025/05/09/hand-posing-basics/)). Anime does the same at a distance: hands become simple shapes, and joint and nail detail appear only in close-ups ([AnimeOutline](https://www.animeoutline.com/how-to-draw-anime-and-manga-hands/)). Together these give a level-of-detail rule for the engine. In wide shots and fast motion, act with whole-hand shapes (fist, open, point) and wrist angle, keep the gaps between fingers clearly open or clearly closed, and let a pointing finger or a held prop break the hand's outline so the pose reads in silhouette. Per-finger stagger and tip joints are kept for close-ups.

The blended hand also removes the fist swap. Today the staff fist is a separate set of rolls that scales up while the open fingers shrink. In one surface, a fist is just a curl of the same fingers, and the fillets follow it. If a hand-sculpted pose is ever needed, the stylized hand packs that swap models make every variant share identical wrist geometry and pivot, so the join never moves ([itch.io low-poly hands](https://kickin-it-studios.itch.io/low-poly-hands-great-for-vr)). The swap should also blend over at least 3 frames, because `qa/motion.mjs` flags one-frame changes.

## A three-step trial settles what the sources could not

Some questions stayed open. No studio document was found for how Pixar, DreamWorks, Nintendo or Arc System Works build or rig stylized hands, nor any measured finger-to-palm ratio for a mascot. The proportions above come from one drawing tutorial and from this report's own reasoning. The per-frame mesh's frame-to-frame shimmer was not measured on rendered frames, and the meshing pass after the optimised field pass was not timed on its own. Several animation sources, including Animation Mentor, Williams' *Survival Kit* and Blair's hand chapter, could be read only through snippets or secondhand summaries. These gaps are why the build should be proven on pictures before it touches a clip.

The trial has three steps, each cheap. First, build the blended glove from the current hand's dimensions and render matching stills of the current and blended hands in the poses the grip library uses (mug, bowl, box, thin, phone, staff), with a pixel-difference sheet as CLAUDE.md's A/B rule asks. Second, render a 15 fps strip of a slow curl and of the straw pinch, and check for a twitching outline at constant pose and during the curl. If it twitches, switch to the bake-and-skin fallback. Third, run `node qa/golden.mjs`, which will change every still with a hand in it, and re-check every storyboard pose that uses a grip, because a new model breaks old poses (CLAUDE.md rule 6). The look decisions are Jonny's and should go to him with the still sheet: three fingers plus thumb instead of the current four plus thumb, cuff or seamless wrist, and the hand's size relative to the head.

## Conclusion

The research turns the problem around. The instinct when a hand looks crude is to add detail, which is how the knuckle balls arrived. But every source, from Blair's mitten to Arc System Works' zero-width glove rims, says hands read as one piece when parts are *shared*, not added. The fix takes parts away: one surface, fewer junctions, one ink shell. The same move helps the contact rules. A hand whose fingers fillet into the palm has no seam lines for the eye to catch when a pad presses a straw. Its invisible skeleton can stay exactly what `stage.js` already measures, so the look changes without touching the checks.

Going from four fingers plus thumb to three plus thumb is also more than a style choice. Each finger removed is one less junction to ink, one less part to curl and one less chance for a finger to cross a prop, which is why the friendliest game mascots sit low on the hand-complexity ladder. A three-finger glove that keeps its fingers together most of the time and separates the index only for a point, pinch or grip gets most of a mitten's simplicity and keeps the four-digit read the cartoon tradition settled on.
